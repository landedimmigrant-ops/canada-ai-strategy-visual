Methodology & transparency

How this was made — the source, the judgment calls, what was verified, and what is interpretation rather than fact. The analytical choices are set out below so they can be reviewed. Read the full strategy ↗

1 · What this is — and isn't

An independent, unofficial visual explorer of a single document: Canada's National AI Strategy, AI for All (ISED). It is not a Government of Canada product and not a substitute for the source — it reorganizes the strategy so it can be explored by audience, money, target and time.

2 · Source

  • One primary source: the official ISED strategy — the web page and its 29-page PDF — published June 2026 (page last modified 2026‑06‑04). Every figure, program, target and date was extracted from that document on June 5, 2026.
  • Corroboration: headline numbers were cross-checked against reputable secondary sources (CBC, The Globe and Mail, BetaKit, BDC, CIFAR, canada.ca) to catch extraction errors.
  • Amounts are in CAD unless noted (one fund is reported in US$).

3 · How the strategy was structured

The prose strategy was hand-coded into structured data so it could be filtered, summed and cross-linked: 3 values · 6 pillars (each with themes and key actions) · 5 priority sectors · 31 concrete programs (each tagged with pillar, audience, dollar amount if any, and status) · 16 named dollar line-items (each flagged new vs. leveraged) · 8 "gap → response" diagnoses · headline targets and baseline→target metrics · a dated timeline (Budget 2025 → 2050).

4 · Definitions & classification rules

The interpretive core, set out so it can be reviewed and contested.

  • Funded vs. unfunded — a commitment is "funded" only if the strategy attaches a named dollar figure to it. Programs described without a specific amount are "unfunded," even if money may flow later.
  • New vs. leveraged — "new" = money the strategy introduces; "leveraged" = existing or previously-announced funds (mostly Budget 2025 and ongoing programs) counted toward the total. Shown distinctly because they're different kinds of commitment.
  • Soft / hedged commitments — flagged where the strategy's own wording qualifies the promise ("where appropriate," "at times," "explore," "assess," "review," or numbers it calls "proposed" / "estimated"). Found by searching the source text for these markers, then reading each in context. Qualified language is not necessarily a weakness, but it is worth surfacing.
  • Live now vs. announced — "live" = running or available today per the source; "announced" = committed but not yet stood up.
  • Pillar / value assignment — each item sits under the pillar the strategy assigns it; pillars roll up to the three values as the strategy describes.
  • Unfunded categories (Law & rights · Standards & trust · Skills & people · Market & structural · Infrastructure & sovereignty · Diplomacy & alliances) are our groupings of unpriced items, not the government's.

5 · Accuracy check

Every figure, target, date, program name and claim was checked line-by-line against the official PDF. It reconciles closely. Notable reconciliations:

  • Priority sectors — the source is internally inconsistent (its sector section enumerates five; a later Workforce-Alliances line says six). We use the authoritative, enumerated five.
  • Wording softened to match the source in three places that read firmer than the document: sovereign compute is "proposed 850 MW → up to 2.3 GW" (the source says proposed / being finalized); mid-career upskilling is "assess and scale up" (the source commits to assessing offerings); Tech Growth Fund equity stakes happen "at times."
  • Estimates are labeled as estimates — e.g. the ~$187B generative-AI figure and the 5.5 GW compute need are projections the strategy itself hedges, not commitments.

6 · Honest caveats

  • "Unpriced ≠ free." Laws, standards work, and tax measures (SR&ED, the super-deduction) carry real cost and effort — "unfunded" here means no named grant, not "costs nothing."
  • Totals are a definitional choice. "$2.36B new / ~$6.2B leveraged" reconciles exactly to the line-items shown; some outlets total the package differently (~$6.6B all-in) by counting previously-announced money against a different baseline. Both can be right — they answer different questions.
  • The Funded / Soft / Unfunded lenses are interpretation, built to make the distance between rhetoric and dollars legible. They're our reading of the strategy, not the government's framing.
  • Dollar amounts are reproduced as the strategy reports them — not independently audited.

7 · Reproducibility

  • Every program, pillar, sector and timeline entry carries a "read more ↗" link to the relevant section of the official strategy, so any claim can be traced to source. (Links land at the pillar/section level — the page's anchors don't go finer.)
  • The whole thing is one self-contained index.html with the data inline, so the full dataset is readable and checkable in the page source.

8 · How it was built

  • A single static HTML file: no framework, no build step, no dependencies, no tracking; works offline; hosted free on GitHub Pages.
  • Built iteratively — starting from the strategy's own architecture (values → pillars), then adding lenses (audience, money, targets, timeline) and finally the Funded → Soft → Unfunded spectrum, tightening accuracy on each pass.

9 · AI assistance — and how accuracy was built

This explorer was researched, structured and fact-checked with AI assistance — Anthropic's Claude (the Claude Opus 4.8 model) — working under human direction and review. Because the strategy is recent, the work was deliberately built to not lean on the model's prior knowledge:

  • Worked from the primary document, not memory. The full 29-page strategy PDF was extracted to text and read directly; figures came from the document itself, not from anything the model "remembered" from training.
  • Independent corroboration. Headline numbers were cross-checked against reputable public sources (CBC, The Globe and Mail, BetaKit, BDC, CIFAR, canada.ca) before being trusted.
  • Line-by-line verification. Every figure, target, date, program name and claim was checked against the PDF (see §5) — which is what surfaced the five-vs-six sector contradiction and the three wordings softened to match the source.
  • A claim-by-claim verification ledger. Beyond this note, every tracked figure, target and commitment is recorded in a structured ledger (ledger.json), each graded and traced to source by a published, reusable method — the Source-Traceable Claim Ledger. Browse it in the Verification tab; the badge on each figure opens its record.
  • Traceability over trust. Every item links back to its section of the official strategy, and the full dataset sits inline in the page — so any claim can be checked rather than taken on the model's word.
  • Build verification. Each change was loaded in a real (headless) browser and checked for correct rendering and zero errors before it shipped.

The honest limit: AI can still make mistakes — misread a figure, miscategorize a program, or carry a subtle bias. The source links, the inline data, and the line-by-line check exist precisely so this work can be audited, not trusted blindly. A human directed the scope and approved every change.

10 · The verification ledger

The accuracy check above was turned into a standing, published artifact: a claim ledger (ledger.json) in which every figure, target and commitment is one row — traced to an exact source quote and labelled by a written, reusable method, the Source-Traceable Claim Ledger. Browse it live in the Verification tab; the badge on any figure opens its record. The point is that you needn't trust this page — you can replay the checks.

Each claim carries two axes: a kind — direct (asserted by the source), derived (computed by us, e.g. a total) or editorial (our framing) — and a basis category set by a deterministic rule (the rubric), so it is reproducible rather than asserted. The basis is a category, not a score:

  • Corroborated: matches the source and an independent outside source agrees.
  • Per the source: a government statement, confirmed word-for-word against the official text.
  • Qualified by the source: the government's own wording hedges, estimates or contradicts itself — verified verbatim; the qualification is the source's, not ours.
  • Recomputed / Our analysis: figures we computed from the data, or our editorial lenses — labelled as ours, not the source's.

How the figures were verified. Because "we checked it" is not a method, every factual claim was put through an independent pass: three separate verifier agents re-fetched the official strategy on their own — blind to the labels — and re-derived each quote from scratch, and the headline dollar figures were corroborated against outside sources (BetaKit, University Affairs, BDC, KPMG, and a Budget 2025 legal analysis). Each record shows its check trail and who ran every step.

Where it stands (verified June 6, 2026): 52 tracked claims — 6 corroborated, 19 per the source, 15 qualified by the source, 6 recomputed, 6 our analysis — with 0 open (none disputed or unchecked). The independent pass also worked in the other direction, which is the real test: it briefly flagged one claim (the conditional "Canadian models could become world-leading on safety") because a summary had skipped the sentence, and a targeted re-read then found it verbatim and cleared it. The ledger keeps that dispute → adjudication → confirm trail in the open — a flag honestly raised and resolved is more trustworthy than a claim never questioned.

What "verified" means here: each claim is faithful to the source and traceable to the exact words — not that the dollars will ultimately be spent, which is a separate question. The one check a machine can't supply is a human's sign-off, which remains the editor's to give.
